# Voice

How Alex writes, learned from his edits when he approves text the agent drafted (constitution
Principle VIII). Read it before drafting anything that will carry his name: a story, a race report,
page wording, a post or CV text. Each approval adds what his edits taught. Style lessons only: no
private facts, no quotes from drafts, no names.

## Sources so far

- **The journey story** (spec 003, approved 4 and 6 Nov 2026): nine chapters, a closing section, the
  "not medical advice" note and the page's wording.
- **The Martlets fundraising story** (spec 002, approved 10 Nov 2026): the "Why I'm running for
  Martlets" text on the fundraising page and JustGiving, written with the family it remembers.

## Writing about someone else's story

- **A personal passage is written by the people it belongs to.** When a story is about someone else's
  family, the agent drafts only the frame (the race, the charity, the facts and figures, the thanks) and
  leaves a marked placeholder with the points to cover. The family writes the passage in their own
  words, and it isn't polished afterwards beyond a missing full stop.
- **Their edits to the frame are kept too.** The family reworded the line after their passage so it
  followed on from it, and the frame took their version. Their text leads; the frame fits around it.
- **It's about the cause, not about him.** Alex kept his own fitness journey out of the fundraising
  story: one line on the race and that it's his first, then the person and the charity.
- **Facts about the charity come from the charity**, quoted from its own pages and re-checked the day
  before publishing, with no figure the source doesn't state.
- **Ask about names and links that drop out.** Before approval, check with Alex whether the text may
  name family members publicly, and whether a link the draft made (why this race) was dropped on
  purpose. Once both were confirmed, the family's version stood.

## Tone

- **Honest about the facts, gentle about himself.** Alex keeps the hard facts of a low point (what he
  weighed, how tired he was, what he was drinking) but cut the harshest self-judgements and a
  defensive denial. Frame a low point as what was missing (just existing, not actually living), not
  as a list of faults.
- **Conversational and British.** Plain words, British spelling, contractions, and the odd
  exclamation or self-aware aside where he'd say it out loud. He kept his own colloquial phrases and
  imagery as he wrote them in answers. Use them; don't tidy them into neutral prose.
- **Warm, not preachy.** Advice comes from what worked for him ("here's what I'd say"), never as
  instruction to the reader, and health advice points people to their GP.

## Structure

- **Every line earns its place.** A trait or detail mentioned in passing, with nothing following from
  it, read as random and came out. Where it pays off later, it can stay there.
- **The lesson follows from the text.** A "What I learned" line must be the main point of the
  chapter's own words, not a side point from elsewhere. Short, in his phrasing, one or two
  sentences.
- **A turning point names each chapter**, and the chapters follow time, with a figure or two that
  supports the words and never leads.
- **Draft the whole thing for one read-through.** He prefers to review everything together at the end
  rather than piece by piece, so drafts should be complete and readable top to bottom, with open
  choices marked inline.

## What stays out

- **A reason he keeps private stays unsaid**, with no hint at it: the text states the effect (the
  motivation went again) and moves on.
- **Other people appear only as he chooses**, unnamed and without detail.
- **Quotes are credited with care**: "usually credited to" or "often attributed to" where the source
  is uncertain.

## Page wording

- **Plain beats clever.** For titles, descriptions, share text and link labels he chose the option that
  says what the page is first, then the arc in his own words, over a quote or a tease.
